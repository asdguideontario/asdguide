Start Here — Do These First

How old is your child? Tap to see what matters most now:

Start with these sections:

    1. Get your OAP number. Register with the Ontario Autism Program through AccessOAP (accessoap.ca). Even while you are waiting for core clinical services funding, having an OAP number opens the door to free Foundational Family Services (below).
    2. Ask for Foundational Family Services. These are free education and support services for parents and caregivers of OAP-registered children — workshops, coaching, and learning sessions. Ask a lead agency (Strides Toronto, Surrey Place, Geneva Centre, or Kerry's Place — see Part A).
    3. Ask for service navigation. Say: "Can a service coordinator help us figure out which programs fit our child?" A coordinator can map services across agencies so you don't have to piece it together alone.
    4. Apply for financial support in parallel — don't wait. Start with the Disability Tax Credit (Part B), because it unlocks other benefits. Then look at SSAH and ACSD. Some supports have waitlists, so earlier is better.

    Sources for this section: Ontario Autism Program (ontario.ca); TDSB — Autism Services & Resources.

    Part B · Financial Support — Funding, Benefits & Tax

    These are programs that provide financial support, reimburse costs, or reduce taxes. The organizations that deliver services are in Part A, kept separate on purpose. Each important program has a “Documents to prepare” list you can open and tick off as you gather papers.

    Before you apply: gather the papers most programs ask for

    Start here for forms

    Many programs ask for the same few documents. Collecting them once saves time:

    • Notice of Assessment (your tax return result) — available in CRA My Account.
    • Social Insurance Numbers for you and your child — how to apply for or find your SIN.
    • Ontario health card and proof of address — documents needed for an Ontario health card.
    • Diagnosis and assessment reports from the professional who assessed your child (keep copies).
    • Receipts for therapy, equipment, camps, and respite you pay for.

    Ontario Autism Program (OAP) funding

    Ontario Autism Program (OAP) — Core Clinical Services Funding

    Ontario · Under 18Invitation in order of registrationAmount set by age and needs

    Funding families use to buy eligible core clinical services such as ABA, speech-language therapy, and occupational therapy. The amount depends on your child's age and assessed needs. When the needs-based model was announced, the yearly ranges were about $10,900–$65,000 (ages 0–3), $8,900–$65,000 (4–9), $7,600–$41,400 (10–14), and $6,600–$31,900 (15–18). ⚠ These ranges may have changed — AccessOAP confirms your child's actual amount.

    How it works: register through AccessOAP, the province's intake organization. Children are invited into core clinical services in the order they registered; a care coordinator then completes a needs assessment to set the funding amount. More children are registered than there is funding for, so the wait can be long and no wait time is guaranteed. ⚠ Reported waits vary — ask AccessOAP about your child's place.

    While you wait, other OAP services are free: Foundational Family Services, caregiver-mediated early years programs (ages 12–48 months), the Entry to School program, and Urgent Response Services (see Part A).

    📞 AccessOAP 1-833-425-2445 · 📧 info@AccessOAP.ca · 🌐 accessoap.ca · ontario.ca/autism

    Start here: the Disability Tax Credit unlocks other financial support

    1 · Disability Tax Credit (DTC)

    Federal · TaxGateway benefit

    A non-refundable tax credit for people with a severe and prolonged impairment, certified by a medical practitioner. Apply for this first — it is the key that unlocks the Child Disability Benefit and the RDSP below. Note: a diagnosis alone does not guarantee approval; the certificate is about how the condition affects daily life. If your child is approved and does not need the full amount to reduce their own tax, the unused portion (including the child supplement) can be transferred to a supporting parent or guardian — ask a tax professional how to claim it.

    📞 CRA 1-800-959-8281 · 🌐 canada.ca — Disability Tax Credit

    2 · Child Disability Benefit (CDB)

    Federal · MonthlyAutomatic if eligible

    A tax-free monthly payment for families caring for a child under 18 with a severe and prolonged impairment. For July 2026 to June 2027 it is up to $3,480 a year ($290 a month) per eligible child, reduced at higher family incomes. If you already receive the Canada Child Benefit and your child is approved for the DTC, you should receive the CDB automatically — no separate application.

    📞 1-800-387-1193 · 🌐 canada.ca — Child Disability Benefit

    3 · Registered Disability Savings Plan (RDSP)

    Federal · Long-term savingsRequires DTC

    A long-term savings plan for a person approved for the DTC, opened at a bank or financial institution. The federal government can add a matching Canada Disability Savings Grant (up to $3,500 a year; $70,000 over a lifetime) and a Canada Disability Savings Bond for lower-income families (up to $1,000 a year with no contribution needed; $20,000 over a lifetime). Both stop after the year the beneficiary turns 49, and money may have to be repaid if it is withdrawn within 10 years — talk to your bank before withdrawing. The beneficiary must have a Social Insurance Number and be a Canadian resident; a plan can be opened until the end of the year the beneficiary turns 59.

    Monthly help & everyday costs

    Assistance for Children with Severe Disabilities (ACSD)

    Ontario · MonthlyIncome-tested

    A monthly benefit for families caring for a child with a severe disability at home, to help with extra costs such as travel to medical appointments, special equipment, and respite. Eligible parents/guardians receive $25–$678 per month (2026 amounts — the maximum is adjusted each July, and the 2026 increase took effect July 1, 2026). Household income must be $77,640 or less, and the individual amount is also set by family size, the severity of the disability, and the family's extraordinary costs.

    Access 2 Card — Your Support Person Gets In Free

    Canada-wide · Easter SealsSupport person freeAll ages

    The Access 2 Card (run by Easter Seals Canada) is for a person of any age with a permanent disability who needs a support person with them at entertainment and recreation venues. Show the card: the cardholder pays regular admission, and their support person — an adult who helps with things like communication, mobility, or personal needs — gets in free, or for no more than $3 at some venues. It is accepted at 500+ participating venues across Canada, including Cineplex movie theatres and many museums, zoos, and attractions (Toronto examples reported include the CN Tower, CNE, AGO and ROM — ⚠ not independently confirmed); check the venue list for Toronto locations before you go.

    Cost & applying: $20 for a 3-year card or $30 for a 5-year card (confirm the current fee when you apply). A registered health care professional must confirm on the application that the applicant needs a support person. Apply online at access2card.ca and allow about 4–6 weeks for the card to arrive. Questions: 1-877-376-6362.

    Part C — Planning Ahead: Turning 18, School's End & Adulthood

    When a young person turns 18, children's programs end — including the Ontario Autism Program, Special Services at Home, and ACSD — and the adult system works differently. Early steps make a big difference. This part covers the timeline, adult income and health coverage, services, school and work, housing, and legal and financial planning.

    Timeline: what to do and when

    1. Around age 14Ask the school to include a transition plan in your child's Individual Education Plan (IEP). ⚠ Confirm the exact requirements with your school team.
    2. Age 16 — apply to Developmental Services Ontario (DSO)You can be confirmed eligible for adult developmental services at 16, even though services start after 18. In Toronto, DSO is managed by Surrey Place; call 2-1-1 or visit dsontario.ca.
    3. Age 16–17Book a transition meeting with your school team.
    4. Age 17½ — apply for ODSPODSP is a separate application from DSO (about six months before the 18th birthday). You can use your DSO eligibility letter as part of it.
    5. Age 18 — legal adulthoodYour child becomes a legal adult. Parents no longer automatically make medical or financial decisions, so look at decision-making options (see "Decision-making at 18" below).
    6. Age 18 — children's services endThe Ontario Autism Program, SSAH, and ACSD end. Adult supports start through DSO-funded services such as Passport, plus ODSP income support.
    7. Until June of the year the student turns 21This is how long a student can attend publicly funded school (see "When school ends at 21" below).

    School, college & work

    When School Ends at 21

    Planning

    In Ontario, a student can attend publicly funded school until the last school day in June of the year they turn 21. When that entitlement ends, the structured school day and school-based supports (such as educational assistants and in-school therapy or behaviour support) stop. Adult developmental services are not an entitlement and often have waitlists, so apply early (DSO at age 16).

    Options families often combine: a CICE college program, day programs, volunteering and recreation (often paid for with Passport funding), and employment programs such as Geneva Centre's Employment Consultation (ages 13+).

    Legal & financial planning

    Decision-Making at 18 (Guardianship & Powers of Attorney)

    Age 18Legal advice needed

    At 18 your child is a legal adult, and parents no longer automatically have authority over medical or financial decisions. In Ontario, capacity is judged decision by decision, not by diagnosis. Options include: if the young adult is capable of it, signing a continuing power of attorney for property and a power of attorney for personal care naming someone they trust; if they are not capable of giving a power of attorney, a guardianship application to the Superior Court of Ontario under the Substitute Decisions Act; and for health care, a substitute decision-maker when a health professional finds the person is not capable of consenting. If an adult is found incapable and has no attorney or guardian, the Public Guardian and Trustee may become the statutory guardian of property. Advice from a lawyer who works with disability planning is important; do this when your child becomes an adult, not later in life. ⚠ This is general information, not legal advice.

    Henson Trust

    Estate planningProtects ODSP

    A Henson Trust is a type of discretionary trust set up (often in a parent's will) to hold money or property for a person with a disability. Because the trustee — not the person — controls whether and when money is paid out, the trust's capital is generally not counted against ODSP's asset limit, and there is generally no limit on what can be put into it. Payments the trustee makes may still affect ODSP depending on what they are used for, so the trust must be drafted carefully and the trustee needs to understand ODSP rules. It works alongside an RDSP (also exempt from ODSP asset rules) — families often use both. Speak with an estate lawyer experienced in disability planning before setting one up; ⚠ details and rules change.

    Glossary — What the Short Forms Mean

    Forms, websites and agencies use many short forms. Here is what the most common ones mean.

    OAP
    Ontario Autism Program — Ontario's program of services and funding for autistic children and youth under 18. You register through AccessOAP.
    FFS
    Foundational Family Services — free workshops, coaching and support for families registered with the OAP.
    ABA
    Applied Behaviour Analysis — a type of therapy often used with autistic children. Ask providers about fees and waitlists.
    DTC
    Disability Tax Credit — a federal tax credit. Approval (using form T2201) can open the door to other benefits, such as the CDB and the RDSP.
    CDB
    Child Disability Benefit — a tax-free monthly payment for families of a child under 18 who is approved for the DTC.
    RDSP
    Registered Disability Savings Plan — a long-term savings plan for a person approved for the DTC. The government may add grants and bonds.
    SSAH
    Special Services at Home — Ontario funding that helps families pay for respite and for help with learning everyday skills.
    ACSD
    Assistance for Children with Severe Disabilities — monthly Ontario payments to help with the extra costs of caring for a child with a severe disability. The amount depends on family income.
    IEP
    Individual Education Plan — a written school plan that describes the special education program and supports your child receives.
    IPRC
    Identification, Placement and Review Committee — a school board committee that decides whether a student needs special education and recommends a placement. Parents can ask for a meeting in writing.
    DSO
    Developmental Services Ontario — the place to apply for adult developmental services. You can apply from age 16.
    ODSP
    Ontario Disability Support Program — income and health benefits for adults (18 and older) with a disability who are in financial need.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    We have an OAP number but no core clinical funding yet. Is anything available to us now?

    Yes. Foundational Family Services — parent education, workshops, and coaching — are free for families registered in the OAP, even while you wait for core clinical services funding. Ask a lead agency (Strides Toronto, Surrey Place, Geneva Centre, Kerry's Place). You can also apply for financial support like SSAH and the Disability Tax Credit in the meantime.

    What is the difference between Part A and Part B of this guide?

    Part A lists organizations that provide services — teaching, therapy, navigation, and support. Part B lists financial support — programs that pay, reimburse, or reduce taxes. They are kept separate so you can find each kind quickly. A few organizations appear in both, once for each role: for example, Geneva Centre runs programs (Part A) and administers respite funding (Part B).

    Which financial support should we apply for first?

    Start with the Disability Tax Credit (DTC). It is the gateway: approval can bring the Child Disability Benefit automatically (if you receive the Canada Child Benefit) and is required to open an RDSP. After that, look at SSAH and ACSD, and join any waitlisted programs — like the Toronto Child Care Fee Subsidy — early.

    My child has a diagnosis. Does that guarantee the Disability Tax Credit?

    No. The DTC is based on how a severe and prolonged impairment affects daily life, certified by a medical practitioner — not on the diagnosis alone. Many children diagnosed with ASD do qualify, but approval is not automatic, and a denied application can often be revisited with more detailed information from the practitioner.

    How can we get a break (respite)?

    Three main routes: SSAH funding can be used toward respite; Family Directed Respite reimburses Toronto families (child 2–17 with an ASD diagnosis, living at home) who are not already using Ministry-funded respite or camp programs; and some providers in Part A, such as Beyond The Spectrum and Mindwerx, offer respite directly. A service coordinator can help you combine these.

    We already use a Ministry-funded camp or respite program. Can we still get Family Directed Respite?

    Possibly not — the Holland Bloorview guide notes that families accessing Ministry-funded respite or camp programs may not be eligible for FDR. Email FDRFunding@autism.net and ask; see the email wording example in "What to Say."

    Is there help paying for camp or sports?

    Yes: Autism Ontario runs two seasonal funds, both by random draw with diagnosis evidence required — the March Break Reimbursement Fund (up to $350 toward a 1:1 support worker or a March Break camp/program) and the One-to-One Summer Support Worker Reimbursement Fund (up to $600 for July–August, for a support worker only, not camp fees; applications ran May 1–31 in the 2026 cycle); Jumpstart offers up to $300 for sport or physical activity; KidSport Toronto up to $250 per year for sport fees; Amici sends children from lower-income households to overnight camp; and Kids in Camp offers camperships through camp directors. See Part B.

    Can we get help in a language other than English?

    Yes. SAAAC serves South Asian families in Urdu, Hindi, Punjabi, Gujarati, Bangla, Tamil, and Arabic. Community Family Services of Ontario offers service in Cantonese, Mandarin, and Vietnamese and can help with subsidy applications. Hong Fook serves East and Southeast Asian communities, and the Chinese Autism Awareness Center offers peer support. See Part A.

    How much does ACSD pay?

    Eligible families receive $25–$678 per month (July 2026 figures — the maximum is adjusted each July). Household income must be $77,640 or less, and the exact amount also depends on family size, the severity of the disability, and extraordinary costs. Details are on Ontario's ACSD page.

    Our child is almost 18. What changes?

    Children's programs end and an adult system begins: ODSP for income support, the Passport program for community participation and support workers, and estate tools like a Henson Trust to protect ODSP eligibility. Start looking a year or more before the 18th birthday — see Part C — Planning Ahead.

    What happens to our services when our child turns 18?

    Children's programs end at 18, including the Ontario Autism Program, Special Services at Home, and ACSD. Adult developmental services go through Developmental Services Ontario (DSO): apply at age 16 to confirm eligibility, and apply for ODSP at about 17½. See Part C for the full timeline.

    How long can a student with special needs stay in school?

    In Ontario, a student can attend publicly funded school until the last school day in June of the year they turn 21. After that, school supports end, so plan ahead — see "When School Ends at 21" in Part C.

    How long is the wait for OAP core clinical funding?

    There is no guaranteed wait time. Children are invited in the order they registered, and more children are registered than there is funding for. Register as early as possible, keep your AccessOAP account up to date, and use the free OAP services (such as Foundational Family Services) while you wait.

    Is this guide official? Can we rely on it completely?

    No — it was created by Joe (Jeong) Han (MSW, RSW) and AI from the sources listed in the Sources section, and it may have errors. It is a starting map, not an official source. Always confirm eligibility, amounts, and deadlines with the organization itself before applying or making decisions.

    Autism Supports in Toronto — Start Here (One-Page Handout)

    Created by Joe (Jeong) Han (MSW, RSW) and AI · November 6, 2026

    ⚠ This handout may have errors. Programs change often — always check details with the organization.

    1. Get your OAP number — registering with the Ontario Autism Program opens free Foundational Family Services even while you wait for core funding.
    2. Ask for Foundational Family Services (free parent education & support): Strides Toronto (Scarborough lead) · Surrey Place 416-925-5141 ext. 2263 · Geneva Centre · Kerry's Place 1-833-775-3779.
    3. Ask for a service coordinator — "Can a service coordinator help us figure out which programs fit our child?"
    4. Apply for the Disability Tax Credit first (CRA 1-800-959-8281) — it unlocks the Child Disability Benefit (automatic) and the RDSP. Then look at SSAH and ACSD (ontario.ca).
    5. For a break: Family Directed Respite funding (Toronto, ages 2–17) — email FDRFunding@autism.net. Autism Ontario (random draw): March Break fund up to $350 (support worker or March Break camp/program); summer support-worker fund up to $600 (support worker only, July–August).
    6. Everyday costs: Toronto Child Care Fee Subsidy 416-338-8888 (waitlist — apply early) · Welcome Policy recreation subsidy · TTC Fair Pass.
